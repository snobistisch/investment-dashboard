01

Three groups, and the dividing line is who still has tokens left to sell

The structural arguments for crypto have not changed and are mostly right. Stablecoins do settle real volume — roughly half of the $320bn outstanding sits on Ethereum. Tokenised treasuries are operational rather than a pitch deck. Perpetual DEXs genuinely take share from centralised exchanges. None of that has been worth owning. Thirty-two of the thirty-four assets here with a full year of history are down, twenty-four of them by 60% or more, while the arguments themselves got stronger. That is the definition of a thesis that is right about the world and wrong about the price.

So the useful question is not which narrative is true. It is what each price already assumes, and how many tokens are still queued up behind it. That second half is where crypto differs from equities: a company cannot print four times its share count on a published schedule, and eleven of the assets here can and will. Grouping by that — rather than by narrative — produces three tiers that behave completely differently, and the grouping is mechanical rather than editorial: it comes straight from fully diluted valuation over circulating market cap.

The tier boundaries are the ones the source research proposed for itself. Applying them to live supply data rather than to remembered supply data moves a lot of names, in both directions.

02

The benchmark is bitcoin, and almost nothing clears it

Crypto research has a habit of measuring itself against zero. Every asset here is a high-beta expression of one factor — global liquidity — and they move together in exactly the conditions where diversification is supposed to help. In that world the question "is this positive expected value in dollars?" is close to meaningless, because in a liquidity upcycle almost everything is. The question that decides anything is whether it beats the simplest, most liquid, most owned thing in the asset class.

So bitcoin is in the ranking as a row rather than in a footnote. On the scenario probabilities in §5 it carries an expected +18.5% per year over three years with the tightest outcome distribution of any asset here. Nine of the other forty-four beat that on raw expected value. One beats it once you divide by dispersion.

That is not a bitcoin-maximalist argument and it is not an argument against owning anything else. It is a statement about what the alternatives have to earn. An asset needs roughly double bitcoin's expected return to compensate for double its dispersion, and on these numbers only Ethereum manages it.

What survives the hurdle test

Three reasons to own something else

A structurally different cash flow. Ethereum's staking yield and Aave's automated buyback are not bitcoin exposure with extra steps — they are claims on activity that exists whether or not the price rises. This is the only category where the argument is genuinely independent.

A drawdown large enough to change the arithmetic. Aave at $1.38bn and 96% float after a 71% fall, Celestia at $293m with 81% float — these are not cheap because the market is wrong, they are cheap because the market gave up. That is still a different bet from bitcoin.

Option value that bitcoin cannot express. Nockchain at a $28m capitalisation is a call on verifiable compute becoming a market at all. It ranks fifth on expected value and thirtieth of forty-five on expected value per unit of dispersion, which is precisely what an option looks like when you measure it honestly.

What does not

The expensive middle

Assets whose only claim is beta. Optimism, Lido, The Graph and Arbitrum are governance claims on ecosystems whose economics do not reach the holder. They carry bitcoin's downside with a fraction of its liquidity and none of its monetary argument.

Winners that already won. Zcash is up 1,165% in twelve months and Hyperliquid 30% — the only two. Both are quality assets and both now price a great deal of good news. They rank 30th and 31st of forty-five here, and neither ranking is a criticism of the protocol.

Products whose token is not the product. Ondo runs a real business that a token holder has no claim on. Aster runs the second-largest perp DEX and returned $12m to holders over a year against a $1.62bn capitalisation. Both carry negative or near-zero expected value here for the same reason.

03

The float rule is right, and it points at different tokens than it used to

The strongest idea in the source research is a rule: do not buy a token where most of the eventual supply has not been issued yet, because the unlock schedule is a guaranteed seller that has nothing to do with whether the protocol succeeds. That is correct, it is unusually falsifiable for a crypto argument, and it is the kind of thing that separates analysis from narrative.

It also has a shelf life, because float moves. A token that was 12% circulating eighteen months ago is not 12% circulating now — the unlocks happened, the price absorbed them, and the overhang the rule was warning about is in the past. Checked against live supply data, the rule reverses on most of the names it was originally aimed at, and lands squarely on a category the source research treated as unavailable.

04

The verifiable-compute companies did not stay private. They listed at 20% float and fell 83%.

The most interesting claim in the source research is that computation itself becomes a tradable, auditable commodity — that zero-knowledge proofs, FHE and trusted hardware turn "trust the API provider" into "verify the output," and that this is what finally makes AI × crypto infrastructure rather than narrative. It then concludes that the good implementations are private and unavailable: "RISC Zero — ZK-prover infrastructure (private; no token yet)", "=nil; Foundation (no liquid token)", "Zama — pre-TGE".

That is no longer true, and it is the single largest factual correction on this tab. RISC Zero's proving marketplace listed as Boundless. Succinct listed. Zama listed in July 2026 and is the largest of them. Aztec, Arcium, Lagrange, Nillion, Irys, Gensyn and Aleo are all liquid. Thirteen verifiable-compute assets are ranked in §5, and the category is not unavailable — it is available, and it has been repriced brutally.

The corrected finding is more useful than the original one, because it is the float rule landing on exactly the cohort that was supposed to be exempt from it. Ten of the thirteen listed at under 30% float. Nine carry FDV above three times market cap. The median is 21% float and 83% below its all-time high. These are not companies that stayed private to protect shareholders. They are companies that sold a fifth of their supply into a bull market and let the other four fifths vest into a bear one.

05

Forty-five assets ranked by expected value, with the arithmetic shown

A composite score out of 100 hides the two things that decide an investment: how big the outcomes are and how likely each one is. This ranking replaces the score with an explicit three-scenario expected value per asset. Every probability below is my judgement and is visible in the table — disagree with a number and you can recompute the ranking yourself, which is the entire point of showing it this way.

Method

For each asset, three scenarios over a three-year horizon to August 2029, with probabilities summing to 1 and a total dollar return in each. There is no earnings multiple to lean on for most of these, so the scenarios are set directly against a price path: what the asset is worth if the thesis compounds, if it merely persists, and if it fails.

EV = Σ pᵢ · rᵢ
σ = √( Σ pᵢ (rᵢ − EV)² )
EVann = (1 + EV)^⅓ − 1

Bull — the thesis compounds and a liquidity upcycle arrives. Base — the protocol persists, adoption grinds, the multiple does not re-rate. Bear — the category does not matter, or the unlock schedule wins. For Tier C the bear case explicitly carries the dilution arithmetic from §3.

What this method cannot do

It is not a forecast and it has no track record. The probabilities are calibrated against nothing except my own reading of each situation. The honest claim is narrower than a prediction: this makes a judgement auditable, comparable across forty-five assets, and falsifiable line by line.

Correlation is not modelled, and here that matters more than anywhere. These are not forty-five independent bets — they are one factor with forty-five tickers. In the bear case they do not fall independently; they fall together, which is exactly when a portfolio needs them not to. EV ranks assets; it does not size a book.

It is mechanically kind to drawdowns. A 90% fall raises expected return arithmetically if the asset survives — and in crypto, unlike equities, "down 90%" is often terminal rather than cheap. The Graph is down 86% and ranks 34th; Aave is down 71% and ranks first. Float quality and revenue do some of that work; judgement does the rest, and judgement is what is on trial here.

Position & moatThe dominant lending protocol in DeFi, and one of a very small number of crypto applications that has survived every cycle since 2020 without a rewrite, a fatal exploit or a rebrand. Lending is the least glamorous and most durable primitive in the category: it works in a bear market, it works with stablecoins as the dominant collateral, and it is the natural venue if tokenised treasuries ever become collateral at scale.

The value accrual is live, not promisedThis is the correction that matters most on this card. Aavenomics 3.0 went live on 27 June 2026: a rules-based engine that routes a fixed share of protocol revenue into open-market AAVE purchases without a governance vote per tranche, currently removing roughly 292 AAVE a day against approximately $400m of annualised protocol revenue. Aave is therefore not a governance token waiting for a fee switch — it is one of the few assets here where usage already converts mechanically into demand for the token. Combined with 96% float, that is why it ranks first: the price fell 71% while the protocol started paying holders.

The bear case, stated properlyThree things, and the first is specific. In March 2026 governance cut the annual buyback budget from roughly $50m to $30m, citing a 25% decline in borrow-fee revenue from its peak — so the mechanism is real but the fuel is shrinking, and a rules-based buyback funded by falling revenue is a smaller bid than the headline suggests. Second, lending is commoditising: Morpho, Spark and a dozen forks compete on the same collateral at lower take rates. Third, simply: an asset that fell 71% in a year can fall 61% again, which is what the 26%-weighted bear case says. Ranking first on expected value is not the same as being safe.

Position & moatThe only asset in this report that clears the bitcoin hurdle on a risk-adjusted basis, and it does so narrowly — 0.80 against 0.77. The argument is the one the source research makes and makes well: roughly half of the $320bn of global stablecoin supply settles here, most tokenised real-world assets are issued here, and staking hit a record 41.7m ETH on 10 August 2026 — about 34.5% of supply — earning a yield that exists independently of price. Those are infrastructure metrics at record levels against a price 62% below its August 2025 high. That gap is the thesis.

The objection that actually bitesNot demand — value accrual. Ethereum spent five years successfully moving activity onto layer twos and making data cheap, and it worked: fees collapsed, which is excellent for users and ambiguous for holders. The bear case is not that adoption stalls, it is that adoption keeps compounding while the asset captures a shrinking share of it. A settlement layer that settles everything for almost nothing is a public good, not a cash flow. My 25%-weighted bear at $1,000 is that argument winning.

What would settle itTwo observable series, both quarterly. Net issuance — whether staking rewards outrun fee burn — tells you directly whether the asset is being diluted by its own security budget. And the share of stablecoin and RWA value that stays on mainnet rather than migrating elsewhere. If the first stays negative and the second holds near half, the fundamentals-versus-price gap is real. If issuance runs positive while the mainnet share drifts below 40%, this is a value trap with excellent infrastructure metrics, and the ranking above is wrong at the top.

If verifiable computation becomes a market at all, a network whose block production is proof production is early to it. That is a real idea and it is why this belongs in a research book at a size that can go to zero without mattering.

The competitive picture has changed, and not in its favourThere is still no verified external customer paying for a proof — the proof market is internal, and the protocol consumes what it produces. What is new since the source research is that the comparison set is now listed and priced. Boundless, Succinct and Lagrange are all trading, with real proving products and, in Succinct's case, production use by rollups. Nockchain is no longer an early option on an unavailable category; it is the smallest and least liquid of five listed proving assets, at $0.6m of daily volume. The first external paying customer is still the event that converts this from a story into a business.

Dispersion of 347% — by a wide margin the widest outcome distribution here; the next is Helium at 193%. Bar capped at 100%.
07

What breaks the thesis — seven ways, each with a falsification trigger

A risk without a trigger is a disclaimer. Each of these carries a specific, observable event that would tell you the risk is materialising while there is still time to act on it — that is the only property that makes a risk list useful.

1 · It is one factor, and it is global liquidity

Every asset here is a levered claim on the same thing. Correlation with equities sits in the 0.6–0.8 range and rises toward 1 in exactly the drawdowns where diversification is supposed to pay. A forty-five-name ranking looks like a portfolio and is a single position.Trigger: a 20%+ equity drawdown in which the median asset here falls further than bitcoin — confirming there is no idiosyncratic support anywhere in the list.

2 · Bitcoin simply wins

The most likely way this exercise turns out to be wasted effort is not that crypto fails, but that bitcoin absorbs the category's entire risk budget. Institutional access exists for BTC and, at a stretch, ETH; it does not exist for the other forty-three. On this report's own numbers only one asset beats bitcoin risk-adjusted.Trigger: bitcoin dominance holding above 60% through a full liquidity upcycle — the alt-season that does not arrive is the falsification.

3 · Value accrual never reaches the token

The recurring pattern across six years: protocol usage compounds, token price does not. Chainlink's usage has grown every year since 2020 and LINK is 83% below its 2021 high. Two live experiments now test the counter-argument — Uniswap's fee switch and Aave's automated buyback — and both are young enough to fail.Trigger: Uniswap's fee switch, live since December 2025 and extended to v4 pools in July 2026, failing to produce a durable re-rating by end-2026. Roughly $23m of 2026 protocol revenue against a $2.2bn capitalisation is not yet obviously enough.

4 · Unlocks decide outcomes regardless of quality

The float rule in §3 assumes dilution is priced gradually. If instead each cliff produces a step down irrespective of fundamentals, Tier C is uninvestable at any price and the entire verifiable-compute cohort in §4 is a value trap rather than a discount.Trigger: Lighter's 27 December 2026 cliff producing a sustained decline while buyback volume is rising. That is the specific, dated, four-months-out test.

5 · The verifiable-compute layer never finds a buyer

The technology is real, listed and liquid — and commercially unproven. Thirteen assets in §4 are priced on a proof market that no participant has yet shown external paying demand for. If none exists by 2029, the category was a narrative with unusually good engineering.Trigger: no proving network publishing verified external revenue above $50m annually by 2029 — and, much sooner, Nockchain's or Boundless's first disclosed external paying customer.

6 · Privacy is regulated out of the listed market

Zcash carries an $8.18bn capitalisation that depends on remaining accessible through tier-one venues. Delisting does not break the protocol — the shielded pool works either way — but it breaks the investment case for anyone who needs a regulated exit.Trigger: delisting from Binance or Coinbase, or two consecutive quarters of declining shielded-pool share, which would remove the usage evidence the thesis rests on.

7 · This ranking is biased toward things that already fell

Stated as a risk because it is one. Expected value mechanically rewards a low base, and in crypto a 90% drawdown is more often terminal than cheap. The Graph is down 86% and ranks 34th; Aave is down 71% and ranks first — the distinction rests on float quality and business durability, which is judgement, not data.Trigger: structural rather than observable. It is a reason to read the drawdown, float and volume columns together, never separately.

08

Five claims that have to be true — and what would change each one

These are not a stacked bull case. H1 and H2 pull against each other: if settlement value accrues cleanly to Ethereum, the application layer captures less of it. Which one you back determines which half of §5 you should be reading.

H1

The 2026 drawdown is a de-rating, not a verdict

The claim the entire ranking rests on. If a 74% median decline reflects liquidity leaving a category whose usage held up, the top of §5 is a discount. If it reflects the market correctly concluding that most of these protocols will never accrue value to their tokens, the ranking is systematically upside-down.

ConfidenceModerate
What would update itProtocol revenue and active-address series against price, quarterly. Usage holding while price falls supports the de-rating reading. Usage falling with price says the market is simply right, and the correct response is fewer names, not cheaper ones.
H2

Settlement is the durable layer, and it is Ethereum

About half of the $320bn stablecoin supply and most tokenised real-world assets settle on Ethereum, 34.5% of supply is staked, and the security budget is paid in the asset itself. This is the best-evidenced claim in the report and the reason ETH is the only asset that clears the bitcoin hurdle.

ConfidenceModerate–high
What would update itNet issuance turning persistently positive — staking rewards outrunning fee burn — or the mainnet share of stablecoin and RWA value drifting below 40% as issuance spreads across chains. Either makes this a public good rather than an asset.
H3

Float, not narrative, explains most three-year returns

The organising rule of this dashboard, and §4 is its strongest evidence: thirteen verifiable-compute assets, median 21% float, median 83% below their highs. If the rule holds, the tier structure in §1 does more work than any thesis in it, and the correct screen is mechanical.

ConfidenceModerate–high
What would update itTier C outperforming Tier A over the next eight quarters. Hyperliquid is the live test and a fair one: the best business in the report sitting in the worst float tier is exactly the case that separates the rule from a superstition.
H4

Verifiable compute is a 2029 market that listed in 2025

Revised from the source research, which said the category was a 2028–2030 opportunity and not yet investable. The second half was wrong: it is investable now. The first half looks right, and the mismatch between a listed cohort and an unbuilt market is what produced an 83% median drawdown.

ConfidenceHigh
What would update itProving cost per inference falling below roughly $0.01, or any network publishing verified external revenue for proofs. The second is the cleaner signal and would move the bull probability on Boundless, Succinct and Nockchain together.
H5

Privacy demand is structural, and already partly paid for

Shielded-pool growth from 11% to 29.9% of supply is real evidence of real usage, and the strongest adoption datapoint in the report. The open question is not whether the demand exists but whether a 1,165% twelve-month move has already collected on it.

ConfidenceLow — stated as an open question
What would update itShielded-pool share continuing to climb through a falling price would confirm the usage is not merely reflexive with the trade. The two moving together is the outcome that says this was a flow story wearing an adoption story.

The one cross-cutting view I hold with reasonable confidence: the structural arguments in the source research are mostly right and mostly not investable through these tokens. Stablecoins are settling real volume, tokenised treasuries are operational, perpetual DEXs are taking share, and verifiable compute now has a dozen listed implementations — and the assets attached to those facts fell by a median of 74% while the facts got stronger. That is not a market failing to notice. It is a market pricing the difference between a protocol working and a token holder being paid for it, which is the distinction the source research identifies as the central lesson of the category and then does not apply to its own top five.
