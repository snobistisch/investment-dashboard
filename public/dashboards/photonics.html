The scarcity moved upstream — from modules to lasers to wafers.

Optical interconnect is the binding constraint on AI cluster scale, and the market knows it. What the market prices less well is where in the chain the scarcity actually sits. Module assembly is crowded and competitive; 200G-per-lane EML lasers and the indium phosphide wafers under them are not. Meanwhile a reported US import ban on Chinese transceivers — announced as a draft five days ago — could redraw 60% of US supply. This maps the full listed universe, not one ETF's basket.

01

One theme, three completely different things to own

The demand case is the least controversial part of this report. Roughly $690–725bn of 2026 hyperscaler capex, up ~77% year on year, with about three quarters of it AI-related, has to move data between accelerators — and copper stops working past about a metre at 200G per lane. Optical transceiver revenue went from ~$16.5bn in 2025 to an estimated ~$26bn in 2026. That part is well understood and largely in the price.

What separates the names is which layer of the chain captures the margin. Module assembly has five credible scale players and a documented history of 60% ASP decay. Laser chips have two Western suppliers at volume. Indium phosphide substrates have fewer still — which is why Nvidia put $2bn of equity into Coherent and why Zhongji Innolight earmarked roughly a quarter of a $6.8bn IPO for laser-chip inventory rather than factories. Follow the scarcity, not the acronym.

Grouped by risk profile rather than by segment, the universe splits three ways, and the three behave differently in a drawdown. The first group already earns; the second is levered to one product cycle; the third is priced on a story.

02

Copper hit a wall at one metre, and the roadmap says so out loud

The bottleneck argument is not a narrative — it is a physics constraint with a published vendor roadmap attached. At 200G per lane, electrical signalling over copper attenuates fast enough that practical reach falls below roughly one metre. As clusters grew from 72-GPU single racks to 576+ GPU multi-rack domains, a rising share of links simply exceeds what copper can carry.

Nvidia has said it will ship both copper and optical for Rubin and Feynman — which is simultaneously the strongest confirmation of the transition and the clearest statement that it is staged, not immediate. Copper stays dominant for ultra-short in-rack reach through at least 2028 on cost and power. That staging is exactly why the copper-side names (Credo's AECs, Astera's Aries and Taurus) and the optical names can both be right for two more years.

06

Half of these companies are each other's customers

The instinct to sort this universe into rivals breaks down fast. Lumentum and Coherent sell the EML lasers into the modules that Innolight, Eoptolink and AAOI assemble — the Chinese module makers are customers of the Western laser names, not only competitors. That is why "US names win, China names lose" is too simple a read on the import-ban story, and why one DSP per module means Marvell's unit volume and Innolight's unit volume are the same number.

Where they need each other

One chain, four handoffs

Substrate → laser chip → module → system. AXT and Coherent sell InP wafers into Lumentum, Yuanjie, LandMark and LuxNet; those sell laser chips into the module makers; those sell modules to Arista, Ciena and the hyperscalers. Fabrinet manufactures across the Western half of that chain and is long all of it.

Copper and optics are complements, not substitutes, until 2028. Astera's Aries/Taurus and Credo's AECs own in-rack reach; optics owns everything beyond roughly a metre. Nvidia shipping both for Rubin and Feynman is the explicit confirmation.

DSPs pair one-to-one with modules. Every pluggable transceiver carries a DSP unless it is LPO. Marvell's optical unit volume is therefore a derivative of exactly the same hyperscaler orders that drive Innolight's — which is why they rarely diverge for long.

06b

A 472x multiple is a bet on the discount rate, and this report had not said so anywhere

The thesis in this dashboard is a physical one — copper runs out of reach above 200G per lane, substrates and lasers are the constraint — and it is well evidenced. The prices attached to it are something else. Trailing multiples across these thirty-three tickers run from 16.6x to 472x, and nothing in the previous eight sections discussed what a change in the discount rate does to that spread. That was the gap.

The mechanism, stated plainly. A high multiple is a statement about when the cash arrives, not only about how much. At 472x trailing earnings, essentially all of the value sits beyond any forecast horizon, so the price is dominated by the rate used to discount it. At 16.6x, most of the value is in cash flows close enough that the discount rate barely moves them. Same theme, same bottleneck, opposite duration.

The July 2026 drawdown is the evidence, and it is already in this repository. SOX fell 28.6% from its 22 June peak; the MS Momentum TMT index fell 53.5% over the same window. The momentum index fell roughly 1.9 times as far as the semiconductor index. The bottleneck did not change in five weeks — no lane rate was revised, no substrate got easier to grow. What repriced was duration and positioning. A reader who holds the 100x+ cohort because the physics is right should understand they were paid, and then unpaid, for something other than the physics.

What this section does not do, and why. It does not model a +100bp move into a price for any name. Doing that needs a cash-flow forecast per company and a defensible terminal assumption, and nothing in this repository supplies either — inventing them would produce a precise number with no support underneath it, which is the failure mode the rest of this dashboard is built to avoid. What is available and is used: the observed multiple spread, the observed forward-to-trailing compression, and one dated market event where duration repriced by 53.5% while the underlying physics did not move at all. That is enough to rank the exposure, which is what a reader needs, and not enough to price it.

07

What breaks the thesis — six ways, ranked by how underpriced they look

None of these is a reason to dismiss the theme. Each is a reason to be specific about which layer you own, because they do not hit the layers equally.

1 · Overcapacity, and it turns financial first

800G output is running an estimated 40–60% below demand through 2027 — exactly the condition that produces overbuilding. 100G ASPs fell ~60% over five years. The financial turn precedes the physical one: margins compress as soon as price declines outrun cost declines and double-ordering unwinds. Stocks can de-rate on 2028 supply while companies are still capacity-constrained. The most underpriced risk in this set, and it lands hardest on the module layer.

2 · Capex growth rate, not capex level

The whole theme is a derivative of ~$700bn of hyperscaler spending. Alphabet's July 2026 results already triggered a sector sell-off on capex scrutiny alone. At 50–60x forward earnings these names do not need spending to fall — a decline in the growth rate is sufficient to compress multiples hard.

3 · China policy, in both directions

The reported FCC draft ban would remove an estimated 60% of US AI data-centre module supply and hit Innolight (~27% global share) first. It is unpublished and may be revised or abandoned. But the mirror risk is real: Western replacement capacity still depends on Chinese indium, China has said it will respond if necessary, and a genuine supply gap raises costs for the US buyers this is meant to protect.

4 · Copper does not roll over on schedule

Copper stays dominant for ultra-short in-rack reach through at least 2028 on cost and power. Every quarter of deferred optical adoption is deferred TAM for the module and laser names — and a direct transfer to Credo's AECs and Astera's Aries/Taurus. Owning both sides is a coherent position; assuming one timeline is not.

5 · Architecture risk inside the winners

LPO removes the DSP. CPO removes the pluggable module. Both are additive to the theme and violently redistributive between these names — Semtech gains where Marvell loses; Broadcom and Nvidia gain where module assemblers and Fabrinet lose. Owning "optics" does not protect you from backing the wrong architecture.

6 · Single-customer dependency, everywhere

Nvidia was 27.6% of Fabrinet's fiscal 2025 revenue. Microsoft was ~29% of AAOI's 2025 revenue. One customer exceeded 70% of Astera Labs' 2025 revenue. These are not diversified businesses, and the concentration is correlated — it is the same four buyers behind almost every name here.

08

Four claims that have to be true — and where price already disagrees

Not a stacked bull case. These compete, and which one you back determines which layer you own. Confidence is my read of the current evidence, paired with the specific thing that would move it.

H1

The bottleneck is upstream, and stays there

Margin accrues to lasers and substrates, not to module assembly. Evidence: Nvidia's $2bn into Coherent's InP line, Innolight spending a quarter of a $6.8bn IPO on component inventory, Lumentum's 200G EML revenue doubling sequentially.

ConfidenceModerate–high
What would update itInP capacity coming online faster than expected — Coherent quadruples by end-2027, AXT doubles — would relieve the scarcity and hand pricing power back to the module layer. Watch Lumentum's 11 Aug and Coherent's 12 Aug prints for EML pricing commentary.
H2

The optical transition is staged, not stepwise

Copper holds in-rack through 2028; optics takes everything beyond. This makes AEC and retimer names complements rather than casualties, and it caps how fast the optical TAM can compound.

ConfidenceHigh
What would update itNvidia pulling CPO forward from Feynman into Vera Rubin Ultra would compress the copper window and re-rate Credo and Astera's copper base downward. A slip in the other direction extends it.
H3

The module layer gets commoditised

Five scale players, 40–60% supply shortfall inviting overbuild, and a documented 60% ASP decay precedent. The bearish corollary of H1: the layer with the most revenue ends up with the least durable margin.

ConfidenceModerate
What would update itModule gross margins expanding through 2027 rather than holding would refute it. AAOI is the cleanest live test — a ~30% gross-margin manufacturer guided to +130% revenue growth. Its Q3 print is the tell.
H4

Policy is now a first-order driver, not a footnote

On 7 August, Coherent moved 13.4% and AXT 17.8% on a regulatory report, not on results. When a draft rule moves an $74bn company double digits in a session, geopolitics has become a primary input to the sector's price.

ConfidenceHigh (as characterisation)
What would update itThe FCC publishing, revising or dropping the proposal — all three are live. A published rule with a long grandfathering window would be far less disruptive than the headline implies; an abrupt ban would hit US buyers through cost before it helps US suppliers through share.

What is genuinely uncertain is which of eight layers keeps the margin, and a basket that owns "photonics" indiscriminately is expressing no view on the only question that matters.
