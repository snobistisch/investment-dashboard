01

Three groups, and the dividing line is who still has tokens left to sell

The structural arguments for crypto have not changed and are mostly right. Stablecoins do settle real volume — roughly half of the $320bn outstanding sits on Ethereum. Tokenised treasuries are operational rather than a pitch deck. Perpetual DEXs genuinely take share from centralised exchanges. None of that has been worth owning. Twenty-three of the twenty-five assets here with a full year of history are down, fourteen of them by 60% or more, while the arguments themselves got stronger. That is the definition of a thesis that is right about the world and wrong about the price.

So the useful question is not which narrative is true. It is what each price already assumes, and how many tokens are still queued up behind it. That second half is where crypto differs from equities: a company cannot print four times its share count on a published schedule, and fifteen of the assets here can and will. Grouping by that — rather than by narrative — produces three tiers that behave completely differently, and the grouping is mechanical rather than editorial: it comes straight from fully diluted valuation over circulating market cap.

The tier boundaries are the ones the source research proposed for itself. Applying them to live supply data rather than to remembered supply data moves a lot of names, in both directions.

02

The benchmark is bitcoin, and almost nothing clears it

Crypto research has a habit of measuring itself against zero. Every asset here is a high-beta expression of one factor — global liquidity — and they move together in exactly the conditions where diversification is supposed to help. In that world the question "is this positive expected value in dollars?" is close to meaningless, because in a liquidity upcycle almost everything is. The question that decides anything is whether it beats the simplest, most liquid, most owned thing in the asset class.

So bitcoin is in the ranking as a row rather than in a footnote. On the scenario probabilities in §08 it carries an expected +18.5% per year over three years with the tightest outcome distribution of any asset here. Eight of the other thirty-nine beat that on raw expected value. One beats it once you divide by dispersion.

That is not a bitcoin-maximalist argument and it is not an argument against owning anything else. It is a statement about what the alternatives have to earn. An asset needs roughly double bitcoin's expected return to compensate for double its dispersion, and on these numbers only Ethereum manages it.

What survives the hurdle test

Three reasons to own something else

A structurally different cash flow. Ethereum's staking yield and Aave's automated buyback are not bitcoin exposure with extra steps — they are claims on activity that exists whether or not the price rises. This is the only category where the argument is genuinely independent.

A drawdown large enough to change the arithmetic, with an economy still attached. Aave at $1.38bn and 96% float after a 71% fall on $860m of trailing fees; Maple at $176m on $108m of fees — these are cheap because the market gave up, not because nothing is happening. That is still a different bet from bitcoin.

Option value that bitcoin cannot express. Nockchain at a $28m capitalisation is a call on verifiable compute becoming a market at all. It ranks fourth on expected value and twenty-fifth of forty on expected value per unit of dispersion, which is precisely what an option looks like when you measure it honestly.

What does not

The expensive middle

Assets whose only claim is beta. Thirteen of these were removed in §4 for exactly this reason. What survives the cull still includes EigenCloud and Lido — real infrastructure whose economics reach stakers and depositors rather than the token.

Winners that already won. Zcash is up 1,165% in twelve months and Hyperliquid 30% — the only two. Both are quality assets and both now price a great deal of good news. They rank 25th and 26th of forty here, and neither ranking is a criticism of the protocol.

Products whose token is not the product. Ondo runs a real business that a token holder has no claim on. Aster runs the second-largest perp DEX and returned $12m to holders over a year against a $1.62bn capitalisation. Both carry negative or near-zero expected value here for the same reason.

03

The float rule is right, and it points at different tokens than it used to

The strongest idea in the source research is a rule: do not buy a token where most of the eventual supply has not been issued yet, because the unlock schedule is a guaranteed seller that has nothing to do with whether the protocol succeeds. That is correct, it is unusually falsifiable for a crypto argument, and it is the kind of thing that separates analysis from narrative.

It also has a shelf life, because float moves. A token that was 12% circulating eighteen months ago is not 12% circulating now — the unlocks happened, the price absorbed them, and the overhang the rule was warning about is in the past. Checked against live supply data, the rule reverses on most of the names it was originally aimed at, and lands squarely on a category the source research treated as unavailable.

04

Thirteen assets removed, eight added, and the two rules disagree about the swap

This revision cuts thirteen assets and adds eight. The cut is defensible on a single number that the previous version did not carry: trailing-year protocol fees. An asset that processes almost no economic activity is not cheap after a 90% fall — it is a claim on nothing, and the expected-value method in §08 cannot tell those apart on price alone.

The additions come from a different argument, and it is worth stating in its own terms rather than smuggling it in. It is the rotation thesis associated with the trader GCR: capital does not return to the assets that just extracted it, it moves to whatever is new — and, in his framing, a team that has not yet been paid is more motivated than one that has. Secondary accounts of his writing put it as the majority trending to zero and being "replaced with new narratives and new rotations". The rotation argument

Capital goes where it has not already been extracted

The case for the new layer ones is not that they earn today. It is that an old token with a five-year vesting schedule and an exhausted holder base has a structural seller and no marginal buyer, while a new one has attention, a motivated team and an unwritten story. On this reading Celestia at $42k of fees is not a bargain at any price, and Monad at $876m of TVL is early rather than expensive.

The evidence for the mechanism is real: over a hundred crypto projects shut down or went dark in the first eight months of 2026, and the general-purpose chains that survived the airdrop-farming era mostly did not keep their users. Rotation is not a mood; it is what the shakeout looks like from the inside.

Where it collides with §3

New means low float, and low float is the rule

The five new additions are, by the float rule this dashboard is built on, the worst assets in the report. MegaETH is 11% circulating at 8.85× fully diluted; Monad 12% at 8.51×; CAP 16% at 6.41×; Superform 22%; Plasma 27%. Three of the five are worse on that measure than Hyperliquid, which §3 already flags as the contradiction inside the source research.

Both arguments cannot be right about the same asset. Either newness outruns dilution, or the vesting calendar wins. This dashboard applies the float rule uniformly — which is why the new layer ones rank 18th, 32nd and 34th of forty rather than in the top ten — and publishes the probabilities so a reader who backs the rotation thesis can raise their base cases and watch them move. That disagreement is the point, not a flaw.

05

The verifiable-compute companies did not stay private. They listed at 20% float and fell 83%.

The most interesting claim in the source research is that computation itself becomes a tradable, auditable commodity — that zero-knowledge proofs, FHE and trusted hardware turn "trust the API provider" into "verify the output," and that this is what finally makes AI × crypto infrastructure rather than narrative. It then concludes that the good implementations are private and unavailable: "RISC Zero — ZK-prover infrastructure (private; no token yet)", "=nil; Foundation (no liquid token)", "Zama — pre-TGE".

That is no longer true, and it is the single largest factual correction on this tab. RISC Zero's proving marketplace listed as Boundless. Succinct listed. Zama listed in July 2026 and is the largest of them. Aztec, Arcium, Lagrange, Nillion, Irys, Gensyn and Aleo are all liquid. Twelve are ranked in §08 — Boundless was culled in §4 for having no measurable fee line, which is a judgement about its economics, not about whether it exists. The category is not unavailable. It is available, and it has been repriced brutally.

The corrected finding is more useful than the original one, because it is the float rule landing on exactly the cohort that was supposed to be exempt from it. Nine of the twelve listed at under 30% float. Eight carry FDV above three times market cap. The median is 21% float and 81% below its all-time high. These are not companies that stayed private to protect shareholders. They are companies that sold a fifth of their supply into a bull market and let the other four fifths vest into a bear one.

06

The stablecoin law pays the issuer and bars the holder. That single clause decides the whole theme.

Until this section existed, the entire repository's treatment of the GENIUS Act was one clause inside an equity row. That is a conclusion with no analysis under it, and the theme is too big for that: on-chain dollars are the only part of crypto with a finished US statute rather than an enforcement posture.

Public Law 119–27, approved 18 July 2025. Senate 68–30 on 17 June, House 308–122 on 17 July. The Act is read here directly rather than through commentary, because the commentary consistently omits the provision that matters most. Reserves must back outstanding coins "on an at least 1 to 1 basis" and the eligible list is a money-market list — cash, demand deposits, Treasury bills, notes or bonds with 93 days or less remaining, overnight repo against those bills, and government money-market funds holding only the above. Then §4(a)(11):

"No permitted payment stablecoin issuer or foreign payment stablecoin issuer shall pay the holder of any payment stablecoin any form of interest or yield (whether in cash, tokens, or other consideration) solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention of such payment stablecoin."

The reserve earns the bill yield. The holder is barred by statute from receiving it. The issuer keeps it. Every second-order effect below is that sentence propagating outward. And §17 carves payment stablecoins out of the definitions of "security" and "commodity" by amending the 1933, 1934, 1940 and Advisers Acts themselves — a definitional carve-out, not a case-by-case exemption, which is why it is worth more than a no-action letter.

Forty assets ranked by expected value, with the arithmetic shown

A composite score out of 100 hides the two things that decide an investment: how big the outcomes are and how likely each one is. This ranking replaces the score with an explicit three-scenario expected value per asset. Every probability below is my judgement and is visible in the table — disagree with a number and you can recompute the ranking yourself, which is the entire point of showing it this way.

Method

For each asset, three scenarios over a three-year horizon to August 2029, with probabilities summing to 1 and a total dollar return in each. There is no earnings multiple to lean on for most of these, so the scenarios are set directly against a price path: what the asset is worth if the thesis compounds, if it merely persists, and if it fails.

EV = Σ pᵢ · rᵢ
σ = √( Σ pᵢ (rᵢ − EV)² )
EVann = (1 + EV)^⅓ − 1

Bull — the thesis compounds and a liquidity upcycle arrives. Base — the protocol persists, adoption grinds, the multiple does not re-rate. Bear — the category does not matter, or the unlock schedule wins. For Tier C the bear case explicitly carries the dilution arithmetic from §3.

What this method cannot do

It is not a forecast and it has no track record. The probabilities are calibrated against nothing except my own reading of each situation. The honest claim is narrower than a prediction: this makes a judgement auditable, comparable across forty assets, and falsifiable line by line.

Correlation is not modelled, and here that matters more than anywhere. These are not forty independent bets — they are one factor with forty tickers. In the bear case they do not fall independently; they fall together, which is exactly when a portfolio needs them not to. EV ranks assets; it does not size a book.

It is mechanically kind to drawdowns. A 90% fall raises expected return arithmetically if the asset survives — and in crypto, unlike equities, "down 90%" is often terminal rather than cheap. Aster earns nothing and ranks 39th; Aave is down 71% and ranks first. Float quality and revenue do some of that work; judgement does the rest, and judgement is what is on trial here. The risk rating beside the EV is a warning, not a correction to expected value.

Risk rating R — a separate warning, not another EV

The expected value above is kind to a zero-or-hero asset, and Nockchain is the case that proves it: a bull case worth ten times the price lifts EV whatever the odds of reaching it, and NOCK came fourth of forty on exactly that arithmetic. R measures what the probabilities do not — how violently the asset moves, how far it has already fallen, how much supply is still to arrive, and whether you could sell it.

R = 0.25·V + 0.25·D + 0.25·F + 0.25·L
V = clamp((volann − 30) / (300 − 30), 0, 1)
D = clamp((|drawdown| − 20) / (95 − 20), 0, 1)
F = clamp((FDV/cap − 1) / (8 − 1), 0, 1)
L = clamp(1 − (log₁₀vol₂₄ − log₁₀0.5m) / (log₁₀50m − log₁₀0.5m), 0, 1)

V and D are measured, not assumed: a year of daily closes per asset from CoinGecko, annualised over 365 trading days, and worst peak-to-trough inside the window. Drawdown runs from the one-year peak rather than the all-time high, for the reason this tab already gives about ZEC — a 2016 listing print is not a price anyone could pay. F and L are read off this table. Buckets: Low below 0.25, Medium to 0.50, High to 0.75, Extreme above.

R does not change a scenario payoff or its probability. Doing that would count the same risk twice when the bear case already contains dilution, illiquidity or failure. Use R to reject or investigate a candidate whose scenario EV looks attractive for the wrong reason.

One decision lens, with its uncertainty left visible:

Scenario-implied EV must clear bitcoin. The new vs BTC column is simply each asset's annualised scenario EV minus bitcoin's. Positive means the published assumptions imply outperformance; it does not mean the market has supplied an objective edge.

Eight of thirty-nine clear that hurdle. They are candidates for diligence, not automatic buys. Nockchain shows why the second step matters: its scenarios imply +2.3 percentage points over BTC, while measured risk is Extreme and daily volume is below the Portfolio Builder's default minimum. The formula exposes the judgement; it does not overrule contrary data.

What breaks the thesis — seven ways, each with a falsification trigger

A risk without a trigger is a disclaimer. Each of these carries a specific, observable event that would tell you the risk is materialising while there is still time to act on it — that is the only property that makes a risk list useful.

1 · It is one factor, and it is global liquidity

Every asset here is a levered claim on the same thing. Correlation with equities sits in the 0.6–0.8 range and rises toward 1 in exactly the drawdowns where diversification is supposed to pay. A forty-name ranking looks like a portfolio and is a single position.Trigger: a 20%+ equity drawdown in which the median asset here falls further than bitcoin — confirming there is no idiosyncratic support anywhere in the list.

2 · Bitcoin simply wins

The most likely way this exercise turns out to be wasted effort is not that crypto fails, but that bitcoin absorbs the category's entire risk budget. Institutional access exists for BTC and, at a stretch, ETH; it does not exist for the other thirty-eight. On this report's own numbers only one asset beats bitcoin risk-adjusted.Trigger: bitcoin dominance holding above 60% through a full liquidity upcycle — the alt-season that does not arrive is the falsification.

3 · Value accrual never reaches the token

The recurring pattern across six years: protocol usage compounds, token price does not. Chainlink's usage has grown every year since 2020 and LINK is 83% below its 2021 high. Two live experiments now test the counter-argument — Uniswap's fee switch and Aave's automated buyback — and both are young enough to fail.Trigger: Uniswap's fee switch, live since December 2025 and extended to v4 pools in July 2026, failing to produce a durable re-rating by end-2026. Roughly $23m of 2026 protocol revenue against a $2.2bn capitalisation is not yet obviously enough.

4 · Unlocks decide outcomes regardless of quality

The float rule in §3 assumes dilution is priced gradually. If instead each cliff produces a step down irrespective of fundamentals, Tier C is uninvestable at any price and the entire verifiable-compute cohort in §05 is a value trap rather than a discount.Trigger: Lighter's 27 December 2026 cliff producing a sustained decline while buyback volume is rising. That is the specific, dated, four-months-out test.

5 · The verifiable-compute layer never finds a buyer

The technology is real, listed and liquid — and commercially unproven. Twelve assets in §5 are priced on a proof market that no participant has yet shown external paying demand for. If none exists by 2029, the category was a narrative with unusually good engineering.Trigger: no proving network publishing verified external revenue above $50m annually by 2029 — and, much sooner, Nockchain's or Boundless's first disclosed external paying customer.

6 · Privacy is regulated out of the listed market

Zcash carries an $8.18bn capitalisation that depends on remaining accessible through tier-one venues. Delisting does not break the protocol — the shielded pool works either way — but it breaks the investment case for anyone who needs a regulated exit.Trigger: delisting from Binance or Coinbase, or two consecutive quarters of declining shielded-pool share, which would remove the usage evidence the thesis rests on.

7 · This ranking is biased toward things that already fell

Stated as a risk because it is one. Expected value mechanically rewards a low base, and in crypto a 90% drawdown is more often terminal than cheap. Aster earns nothing and ranks 39th; Aave is down 71% and ranks first — the distinction rests on float quality and business durability, which is judgement, not data.Trigger: structural rather than observable. It is a reason to read the drawdown, float and volume columns together, never separately.

Five claims that have to be true — and what would change each one

These are not a stacked bull case. H1 and H2 pull against each other: if settlement value accrues cleanly to Ethereum, the application layer captures less of it. Which one you back determines which half of §5 you should be reading.

H1

The 2026 drawdown is a de-rating, not a verdict

The claim the entire ranking rests on. If a 66% median decline reflects liquidity leaving a category whose usage held up, the top of §08 is a discount. If it reflects the market correctly concluding that most of these protocols will never accrue value to their tokens, the ranking is systematically upside-down.

H2

Settlement is the durable layer, and it is Ethereum

About half of the $320bn stablecoin supply and most tokenised real-world assets settle on Ethereum, 34.5% of supply is staked, and the security budget is paid in the asset itself. This is the best-evidenced claim in the report and the reason ETH is the only asset that clears the bitcoin hurdle.

What would update itNet issuance turning persistently positive — staking rewards outrunning fee burn — or the mainnet share of stablecoin and RWA value drifting below 40% as issuance spreads across chains. Either makes this a public good rather than an asset.

Float, not narrative, explains most three-year returns

The organising rule of this dashboard, and §4 is its strongest evidence: twelve verifiable-compute assets plus five new layer ones, median float near 21%, median 81% below their highs. Verifiable compute is a 2029 market that listed in 2025

Revised from the source research, which said the category was a 2028–2030 opportunity and not yet investable. The second half was wrong: it is investable now. The first half looks right, and the mismatch between a listed cohort and an unbuilt market is what produced an 81% median drawdown.
