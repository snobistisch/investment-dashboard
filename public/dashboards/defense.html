The rearmament trade already happened. Then it fell 40%.

Every brief on this theme opens with the budget numbers, and the budget numbers are real: NATO's floor moved from 2% of GDP to a 3.5% core commitment by 2035, and Europe now spends more on defence than at any point since the Cold War. What almost none of them mention is that the median name in this universe trades 24% below its 52-week high, and the high-beta half — Rheinmetall −43%, AeroVironment −53%, DroneShield −68% — has been in drawdown for months while the cash-return primes sit at record highs. That inversion, not the budget line, is where the expected value now sits.

01

One budget cycle, three completely different things to own

The demand case is the least controversial part of this report, and it is also the part that is already in the price. Global military spending passed $2.7 trillion in 2024 and has grown every year since 2015; at the June 2025 Hague summit NATO members committed to 3.5% of GDP on core defence plus 1.5% on defence-related infrastructure by 2035, which is the largest formal step-up the alliance has ever agreed. European defence stocks re-rated violently on that, and then gave much of it back. Rheinmetall is a five-bagger from 2022 that is also 43% below its 52-week high.

What separates the names now is not who benefits from the budget — almost all of them do — but what each price already assumes. A 21x forward multiple on Rheinmetall after a 43% drawdown is a different proposition from 28.5x on RTX at an all-time high, and both are different again from 75.6x on Palantir or 70.6x on Mercury Systems. Every ranking in §5 is built to make that difference explicit rather than to compress it into a score.

Grouped by how the valuation is anchored rather than by weapons category, the universe splits three ways, and the three behave completely differently in a Ukraine-settlement scenario. The first is anchored to a funded backlog; the second to a program that has to be won; the third to a story.

Tier A · Backlog-anchored

Order books that already exist

Profitable, with multi-year funded backlog and, mostly, dividends. The valuation rests on delivering contracts that are already signed. You give up torque: these do not triple on a good headline. Forward multiples span 9.2x (KBR) to 43x (Elbit) — the tier is defined by earnings quality, not by cheapness.

02

Two theses wear the same clothes, and they do not pay off together

Most defence research — including the brief this dashboard was built from — opens with the autonomy argument and then recommends European industrials. Those are two different bets, and being explicit about which one you are making is the single most useful thing this section does. The autonomy thesis says cheap attritable mass displaces exquisite platforms. The rearmament thesis says the existing platforms get ordered in far larger quantities. Ukraine is cited as evidence for both, which should be a warning.

They diverge on the thing that matters most: a Ukraine settlement is close to fatal for one and merely a headwind for the other. If the war ends, the volume argument for artillery, air defence and armour weakens immediately; the argument that armies must restructure around autonomy does not. Conversely, an autonomy-led restructuring is bad for the tonnage names even in a war scenario, because a euro spent on 100,000 interceptor drones is a euro not spent on a Leopard.

Thesis A · Autonomy & attritable mass

Cost-per-effect inverts

The claim. A $500 first-person-view drone that disables a $5m armoured vehicle changes the arithmetic of ground combat permanently, and militaries reorganise procurement around volume manufacturing, software iteration and consumable munitions rather than 30-year platforms.

What has to be true. Programs of record — not experiments — have to move to non-traditional suppliers at scale, and the winners have to manufacture at battlefield cost, not defence-prime cost. This is a manufacturing thesis dressed as a technology thesis.

Listed expression, and its problem. AVAV · KTOS · RCAT · DRO · UMAC · PLTR — a short list, mostly sub-scale, and it excludes the companies that actually embody the thesis. See the private-market note below.

Thesis B · European & allied rearmament

Volume, not novelty

The claim. Two decades of underinvestment left NATO with empty magazines and a hollowed industrial base. Rebuilding it is a 10–15 year capital cycle in ammunition, air defence, armour, submarines and the machine tools behind them — largely with existing product designs.

What has to be true. Budgets have to convert into signed multi-year contracts rather than announcements, and capacity has to be added without destroying margin. This is a backlog thesis, and backlog is observable — which makes it the more falsifiable of the two.

Listed expression. RHM · KOG · R3NK · TKMS · LDO.MI · HO.PA · IDR.MC · FCT.MI · SAAB · BAB.L · CHG.L · AVIO — deep, liquid, and mostly profitable. The market has far more ways to express B than A.

The structural problem with Thesis A: its best companies are private. Anduril, Helsing, Shield AI, Saronic and Castelion are the reference implementations of autonomy-first defence, and none of them is investable on a public exchange. The listed proxies are therefore either incumbents with an autonomy option attached (Rheinmetall's Skyranger, Kongsberg's loitering work, Leonardo DRS) or sub-scale pure-plays whose main financing instrument is equity issuance. This is not a gap in the research — it is a property of the market. Anyone who tells you they have clean listed exposure to autonomy-first defence is describing a portfolio of second-best substitutes, and should say so.

03

Four bottlenecks, and only one of them is drones

The useful question in a supply-constrained cycle is not who has the best product but who owns the step that cannot be scaled quickly. In defence right now those steps are chemical, optical and human — energetics, image intensification, and welders with security clearances. None of them is a software problem, and all of them have two-supplier or one-supplier economics somewhere in the chain.

Bottleneck 1

Energetics & solid rocket motors

Every interceptor, every guided round. Propellant, nitrocellulose and TNT capacity is the hardest physical constraint in NATO, with permitting and safety-case timelines measured in years rather than quarters. The listed pure-plays are thin and mostly expensive.

LHX (Aerojet) · KRMN · AVIO · CHG.L · Nammo, Eurenco, Nitro-Chem — private/state
Bottleneck 2

Night vision & image intensification

The least-discussed near-monopoly in European defence. Image intensifier tube capacity sits with a very small number of qualified producers, and every NATO soldier-modernisation program consumes it. Asset-light, high-margin, and directly geared to troop-numbers rather than to platform counts.

EXENS · THEON · TDY (FLIR) · ESLT
Bottleneck 3

Counter-UAS sensing

The genuinely new problem. Defeating cheap mass requires cheap distributed detection, not exquisite radar — the cost-exchange ratio has to work at the interceptor level or the defender loses by winning. Radar, EW and effector integration is where this is decided.

HAG.DE · RHM (Skyranger) · KOG · DRO.AX · DRS · MRCY
Bottleneck 4

Naval & nuclear labour

Submarine and surface-combatant capacity is constrained by shipyards and cleared welders, which cannot be bought at any price on a three-year horizon. AUKUS and European submarine programs run against the same wall. Slow, unglamorous, and almost impossible to disintermediate.

BWXT · GD · BAB.L · TKMS · FCT.MI · 012450
Not a bottleneck

Airframes and autonomy software

Flagged deliberately. Small-UAS airframes are commodity manufacturing with Chinese cost leadership, and autonomy software has many credible suppliers. Value in the drone stack accrues to secure components, datalinks, munitions and — mostly — to whoever holds the program of record.

AVAV · KTOS · RCAT · UMAC · ONDS
Contested

Command & control software

Palantir has demonstrated the model works and is priced accordingly at 75.6x forward. The interesting question is whether the incumbent integrators — trading at 11–18x forward with the same customers and the same clearances — capture the follow-on work or lose it.

05

Fifty-three names ranked by expected value, with the arithmetic shown

A composite "score" out of 100 hides the two things that decide an investment: how big the outcomes are and how likely each one is. This ranking replaces the score with an explicit three-scenario expected value per name. Every probability below is my judgement and is visible in the table — disagree with a number and you can recompute the ranking yourself, which is the entire point of showing it this way.

Method

For each name, three scenarios over a three-year horizon to August 2029, with probabilities summing to 1 and a total return (price plus dividends) in each. Returns are built from two drivers: earnings growth to 2029, and the multiple the market pays at the end versus the forward multiple it pays today.

r = (E2029 / E2026) × (Mexit / Mnow) − 1
EV = Σ pᵢ · rᵢ
σ = √( Σ pᵢ (rᵢ − EV)² )

For loss-making names where a multiple is meaningless, the scenario returns are set directly against a revenue-and-margin path to 2029 and flagged n/m in the forward-P/E column. Bull = thesis compounds and the multiple holds. Base = growth decelerates to program pace and the multiple normalises. Bear = a Ukraine settlement, a budget slip or an execution failure, with a de-rating on top.

What this method cannot do

It is not a forecast and it has no track record. The probabilities are calibrated against nothing except my own reading of each situation. The honest claim is narrower than a prediction: this makes a judgement auditable, comparable across 53 names, and falsifiable line by line.

The correlation problem is not solved here. These are not 53 independent bets. The top ten share one driver — European and allied budget conversion — so a portfolio built by taking the top of this list would be a single, very concentrated factor position. EV ranks names; it does not size them.

Two lenses, two answers. Sort by EV and the deep-drawdown names win, because a 45% drawdown mechanically raises expected return if the thesis survives. Sort by EV/σ and the cheap, unglamorous names win: Leidos, QinetiQ, L3Harris and Rheinmetall are the only four above 1.0. Which column you believe depends on whether you are paid on outcome or on path.

Position & moatThe only company in Europe that spans the whole rearmament budget rather than a slice of it: artillery ammunition and propellant, armoured vehicles, air defence, and — through Skyranger — the gun-based counter-UAS effector that fixes the cost-exchange problem in §3. Why it ranks firstNot because the story is best. Because the price fell 43% while the backlog did not. The bear case is priced at 20% in my scenarios and it is a real one — a Ukraine settlement plus German budget slippage takes the shares to roughly €740. Third, and least discussed: Rheinmetall is the highest-EV name in this report and simultaneously the most exposed to the single scenario that hurts the most names at once. What would change my mindTwo things, and both are observable. First, US government revenue growth sustaining above 40% year on year for four consecutive quarters would mean the defence budget is converting faster than my base case assumes and the multiple is defensible. Second, evidence that the incumbent integrators — Leidos at 10.9x, Booz Allen at 11.7x, CACI at 17.8x, all with the same customers and clearances — are losing follow-on work to Palantir rather than merely being outgrown. The 65x multiple gap between Palantir and Leidos is the single largest valuation dispersion in this report, and one of the two prices is wrong. I do not have high confidence in which.

EV/σ of 0.43 — the widest outcome distribution of any company above $100bn in this report.
07

What breaks the thesis — seven ways, each with a falsification trigger

A risk without a trigger is a disclaimer. Each of these carries a specific, observable event that would tell you the risk is materialising while there is still time to act on it — that is the only property that makes a risk list useful.

1 · A Ukraine settlement, and the second-order budget effect

The most underweighted risk in almost every defence brief, including the one behind this dashboard, which rated it "low-medium". A negotiated end does not cut budgets immediately — replenishment continues for years — but it removes the political urgency that converts announcements into signed contracts, and multiple compression happens on the announcement, not on the cash flows. This hits Thesis B hard and Thesis A far less.Trigger: a ceasefire holding 90 days, followed by any two of the top five European defence budgets revising 2027 plans downward.

2 · The re-rating unwinds further, not the earnings

The whole complex is priced on multiple expansion that already happened. The 2026 drawdowns are evidence that this process has started and is not finished — and multiple compression does not need bad news, only the absence of new good news. At 21–30x forward, the European names have roughly 30% of downside available from de-rating alone with earnings entirely intact.Trigger: sector forward multiples compressing while order intake grows — de-rating on good news is the signal that the marginal buyer is gone.

3 · Capacity added at the peak

Every major European producer is expanding ammunition, propellant and vehicle capacity into what governments describe as a permanent step-change. If it is instead a replenishment spike, that capacity carries fixed cost into a trough around 2029–2032. The industry has done exactly this before, after 1991.Trigger: any large-cap European producer reporting gross-margin compression on rising revenue for two consecutive halves.

4 · Procurement inertia beats the disruptors

Thesis A depends on non-traditional suppliers winning programs of record, not demonstrations. Defence procurement is structurally hostile to that: qualification cycles, cost-accounting standards and incumbent lobbying all favour the primes, and the most credible challengers are private and can stay private.Trigger: no non-traditional supplier winning a $500m+ program of record within three years — or, inversely, a prime acquiring the leading challenger unchallenged.

5 · The drone stack commoditises faster than it scales

Attritable means cheap, and cheap means low gross margin. If the volume thesis is right about units and wrong about price, the drone names grow revenue into declining margins — the classic hardware trap. Chinese component cost leadership sets the ceiling even where Chinese supply is excluded.Trigger: AeroVironment, Kratos or Red Cat reporting two consecutive quarters of gross-margin decline on rising unit volumes.

6 · US posture reverses the geography of the trade

A material US force reduction in Europe would accelerate European procurement — and simultaneously make "buy European" instruments harder for US primes, which is why the geographic split in this report is not decoration. The mirror risk is a US administration pressuring allies toward US equipment as a condition of security guarantees, which would reverse it.Trigger: either a 30%+ announced US force reduction in Europe, or a NATO member cancelling a European program in favour of a US one under explicit political pressure.

7 · Concentration inside the ranking itself

The method in §5 ranks names independently and they are not independent. The top ten share one factor: European and allied budget conversion. A book built from the top of this list would look diversified by ticker and be a single position by exposure — and risks 1, 2 and 3 would all hit it simultaneously.Trigger: this one is structural rather than observable. It is a reason to distrust the ranking as a portfolio, not a reason to distrust it as a ranking.

08

Five claims that have to be true — and what would change each one

These are not a stacked bull case. H1 and H3 compete: if autonomy takes procurement share as fast as H3 assumes, some of the tonnage spending in H1 does not happen. Which one you back determines which half of §5 you should be reading.

H1

European budget growth persists independently of Ukraine

The load-bearing claim for two thirds of this universe. Evidence for it is unusually good: Germany exempted defence spending above 1% of GDP from its constitutional debt brake in March 2025 — legislation, not a communiqué — and the NATO 3.5% core commitment was agreed by 32 governments in June 2025.

ConfidenceHigh
What would update itA settlement followed by any large member missing its own published trajectory. Watch national budget submissions rather than summit language: the gap between committed and appropriated is where this claim actually gets tested.
H2

The bottleneck is chemical and physical, not digital

Propellant, energetics, image-intensifier tubes and cleared shipyard labour gate delivery; software does not. This is why §3 ranks the segments the way it does and why the report is more positive on energetics and optics exposure than on airframes.

ConfidenceModerate–high
What would update itMunitions lead times normalising toward pre-2022 levels, or any major producer reporting that it is demand-constrained rather than capacity-constrained. Either would move margin power back to the integrators.
H3

Attritable autonomy takes a double-digit share of procurement

The claim underpinning Thesis A, and the weakest-evidenced one in this report. Battlefield adoption is not in doubt; the transfer of budget authority from platform programs to consumable autonomous systems at scale is. Militaries have restructured around a new weapon class before, but rarely inside a decade.

ConfidenceModerate
What would update itA named program of record above $1bn awarded to a non-traditional supplier, or a major army publishing a force structure that replaces a platform program with an autonomous one. Absent that, treat the drone names as options rather than as compounders.
H4

The 2026 drawdown is a de-rating, not a demand signal

The claim the entire EV ranking rests on. If the 24% median drawdown reflects multiple compression against intact order books, the top of §5 is a discount. If it is the market correctly anticipating a settlement and slower budget conversion, the ranking is systematically upside-down.

ConfidenceModerate
What would update itOrder intake and book-to-bill, reported quarterly, against price. Backlog growing while shares fall supports the de-rating reading; backlog flattening supports the other one. This is the single most checkable claim in the report and it should be re-checked every quarter.
H5

Defence software is one market at two prices

Palantir at 75.6x forward and Leidos at 10.9x sell overlapping capability to the same customers under the same clearances. Either the incumbents are structurally impaired and deserve a value multiple, or Palantir's premium prices a share shift larger than the evidence supports.

ConfidenceLow — stated as an open question
What would update itRecompete outcomes. Watch whether Leidos, CACI, Booz Allen and Parsons retain their large mission-software recompetes over the next eight quarters. Their contract-award announcements resolve this faster than either company's revenue growth does.

09

Where to point due diligence next — mapped to which claim you back

Not a buy list. The ranking in §5 orders names; it does not build a portfolio, and the correlation problem in risk 7 means the top of the list is one bet wearing ten tickers. What to diligence depends on which of the five claims in §8 you actually believe, and the three routes below imply genuinely different books.

If you back H1 + H4 · Rearmament at a discount

Buy the drawdown, size it small

RHM · R3NK · KOG · FCT.MI
context: 012450, 272210, 079550

Diligence order intake and book-to-bill first — that single series resolves H4. The trap is treating four names as diversification when they share one driver. This is the highest-EV route and the least diversified one in the report.

If you back H2 · The bottleneck is physical

Own the input, not the platform

LHX · CHG.L · EXENS · THEON
watch: KRMN, AVIO — right asset, hard price

The best risk-adjusted route: L3Harris and QinetiQ sit above 1.0 on EV/σ, and energetics and optics are less settlement-sensitive than tonnage. Diligence capacity-expansion timing and, for Karman and Avio, whether the multiple leaves anything for the buyer.

If you back H3 · Autonomy displaces platforms

Accept that the market is second-best

AVAV · KTOS
options only: RCAT, DRO · avoid: UMAC, ASTS

The purest expressions — Anduril, Helsing, Shield AI — are private, so the listed route is two mid-caps at 44x and 57x forward after 50%+ drawdowns. Diligence gross margin, not revenue growth: the whole bear case is that attritable means low-margin.

The one cross-cutting view I hold with reasonable confidence: the budget is not the variable here. European and allied spending growth over the next five years is about as well-supported as a macro claim gets — legislated in Germany, committed at NATO, and visible in order books. What is genuinely uncertain is how much of it is already in the price, and the honest answer differs by name rather than by theme. A basket that owns "defence" indiscriminately at these levels is expressing no view on the only question that matters, and is buying the primes at their highs to do it.
